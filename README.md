# Homecoming Hero

A first-person running back game you play with your body. Prop a phone or tablet in front of you, take the handoff, and sprint, juke, truck, and stiff arm your way through a four-quarter homecoming game. On 3rd and long, you stay in to pick up the blitz or run a route out of the backfield.

Part of the [body arcade](https://github.com/hungkach/body-arcade): effort is rewarded, and the whole experience stays accessible. A tired, honest rep always counts; higher knees, bigger bounds, and deeper squats pay more.

Play at **https://hungkach.github.io/homecoming-hero/**. Camera play needs that https link. Touch play works anywhere.

## Controls

| Move | Body | Touch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Take the handoff | Start sprinting at the snap; the QB meets you at the mesh (or pitches on a toss) | Tap fast |
| Sprint | High knees in place. Faster knees run faster, and higher knees add a little more; tired, lower knees still count | Tap fast |
| Find the hole | Automatic: past the line you bend toward open grass. Behind it, zone runs are your call: no bound and you bang it (the open crease between the center and the play-side tackle); bound toward the play side to bounce it outside, away from it to bend it back. The green arrow points at the most open lane; your pick turns it gold. Once you cross the line a bound is a juke. Other runs follow the most open lane on their own. When a defender squares you up, a gold ring and a beep mark him and the path stops: your move | Automatic; on zone runs swipe sideways behind the line to bounce or bend |
| Juke | Bound out to one side and straight back. A bigger, faster bound cuts farther (up to about 2.5 yards) and fools even a tackler who is under control | Swipe sideways |
| Goal line dive | Inside the 3: jump at the line to go over the top. Leave from the pile, not early; speed carries you farther | Swipe up (it replaces the stiff arm there) |
| Truck | Duck your head and shoulder, or squat; it stays lowered for the next 2.5 yards. Works on a tackler in front of you (10 to 2 on a clock, 12 where you're running) | Swipe down |
| Hurdle | Jump. You go over a tackler in front of you (10 to 2); the landing costs a little speed. Same jump as the catch | Two-finger tap |
| Stiff arm | Raise one arm out to the side the tackler is on; it works as long as you hold it out. Works on a tackler at your side (2 to 5 on the right, 7 to 10 on the left); head on, it does little. On a breakaway, when one man closes from the side and nobody else is around, your view glances at him and that edge flashes red with a hand | Swipe up |
| Pass pro: set and anchor | Squat before the blitzer gets there (any depth; deeper fills the bar faster), then hold it about 2.5 seconds (you step into his lane on your own) | Hold a thumb down |
| Pass pro: punch (bonus) | Both arms out to the sides as he hits: he's stunned, the hold is shorter, the pocket is cleaner | Tap |
| Route: run it | High knees | Tap fast |
| Route: break | Automatic at the cone; keep the knees up | Swipe toward the break |
| Route: catch | Jump (or both arms up) as the ring closes | Swipe up |

Before every snap: in the huddle the QB gives the official call ("Ace right, 24 zone, on one. Ready, break!") while a big card shows the call, the play in plain words, and the three things to do; the huddle breaks with a clap and the card clears; the offense jogs to the line while you walk up; you scan the defense left and right while the QB identifies the front ("Nickel! Nickel!", "Mike's hot!", "Mike 52!"); then a real cadence ("Down! Set! Blue 42!" and the hut, or two huts on a hard count). Each step lasts as long as its words, so the rest between plays stays short. The booth jokes mostly after plays, and now and then while the offense lines up. Nobody talks over anybody: important calls wait their turn and small ones are skipped. (The 3D view shows the huddle and lineup; the timing is the same in 2D.)

Run blocking gives every defender in the box a blocker, Madden style, and every block is a strength contest: two linemen on a down lineman drive him back, then one peels off to a linebacker (a combo block), and a block that is losing gives ground and breaks sooner. When the defense has one more man than the offense has blockers, the one left free is the man farthest backside, and he has to check the cutback before he chases. Blockers aim between their man and you, not at where he stands, and the line holds about 3 seconds. Receivers block the corner over them; the safeties stay free to fill the alley, so a long run still needs a move. Each scheme: zone steps playside together and climbs to the linebackers, power down-blocks and pulls the backside guard through the hole, the toss pulls the playside guard and tackle out in front, and the dive drives straight ahead. Receivers stalk-block, linebackers read the play before they fill, and a blocked defender fights across his blocker's face and can grab you as you go by. Lanes open where the blocks win, and a green arrow marks the most open one (bang, bend, or bounce on zone) until you reach the line. Running into the back of a block slows you and slides you off it. Fight for yards is earned: if you go down while trucking, or while running fast (about 3.4 steps a second or more), it becomes a pile, and fast knees with a low shoulder drive it up to about 3 more yards. Go down slow or standing and the play is just over. Stay low (squat or duck) and churn fast in the pile, or hit the pile in a deep squat and just churn, and you go beast mode: you burst out the other side, still running. At full flight (about 5 steps a second) you run through almost anyone; one tackle in ten still holds, and then you get the pile.

Play calls follow the situation: power and the dive in short yardage and at the goal line, tosses on long yardage, pass pro or a route on 3rd and long, more passing when trailing late, and never the same play twice in a row. On 4th down the coach goes for it on 4th and inches, on short yardage in Raiders territory, or when trailing late; otherwise it's a field goal (50 yards or less) or a punt, and field position changes the Raiders' odds. The Raiders' front follows the situation too: goal line (packed in, no one deep), nickel on 3rd and long (light box), cover 2 on 2nd and long, and the occasional run blitz. The play call tells you what they're in.

Pass pro is a wall sit: be set before he arrives, then hold through the bull rush while the bar fills. Hold 80 percent of it for a clean pocket, half for a hurried throw; stand up early and it's a sack. Before the snap, the real blitzer creeps closer and sinks lower; the other one bounces in place, then drops into coverage.

Routes: the route is drawn on the grass before the snap, with an orange cone at the break. A linebacker covers you. Break on time and he bites, which opens space for the catch. The ball's whoosh rises to its peak right as it arrives, so you can time your hands by ear. After the catch it's a normal run.

More ways to get the ball in space:
- Outside zone (18 and 19 stretch): everyone reaches toward the sideline; press the edge, then cut up behind the block that wins, or bounce it outside.
- Halfback screen: sell the block for a beat while the rush runs past you, drift to the flat, catch the short throw, then follow the linemen who release in front of you. It shows up on early downs too.
- Halfback motion: before the snap you jog out to the slot (a few seconds of rest), then run a slant, an out, or the seam against the defender over you. The seam has no break: keep running and the ball comes over your shoulder.

## After the game

The final screen states what you did: the score, minutes played, seconds at sprint pace (3 or more steps a second), plays, carries, rush yards, long run, touchdowns, first downs, tackles broken by juke, truck, stiff arm, or speed and power, catches, receiving yards, and pass pro reps with clean pockets, hurried throws and sacks. In body play it also keeps records on this device, in body units, each shown with your previous best: top cadence, highest knees (thigh angle), longest juke bound and deepest truck squat (both in torso lengths). Your fitness is the progression: rewards scale with your actual output, never rescaled to your own best.

## Replays

A touchdown or a run of 20 yards or more gets a TV replay in the break before the next snap: a high angle behind you at 70 percent speed, with your blockers and their defense as they really moved (3D view only). The booth calls it. It doubles as a few seconds of rest.

## Pacing

There's no heart rate monitor, so the game estimates how hard you've been working from your recent live seconds. After a big play (15 yards or more) or an unusually hard stretch, the next play sneaks in a little rest: a receiver goes in motion before the snap (your eyes follow him across, about 2 extra seconds), and the booth is more likely to get a joke in while the offense lines up. When run after run gets stuffed, the defense eases off a little (slower reads, a step slower) until you break one again. Nothing gets harder for playing well. A touchdown already gives you the Raiders' possession to rest.

## Workout length

Pick 15, 20, 25, or 30 minutes on the start screen. The scoreboard still shows four 5 minute quarters, but the game clock follows your real workout time, so the final whistle lands on the length you picked (give or take the last play). Breaks count; pauses don't.

## Startup (every session, about a minute)

1. **Framing check.** Live camera view with spoken coaching (step back, step closer, step left or right, tilt up or down) until you fit with a little margin. Full-body setups also check that your hands fit overhead.
2. **Warm-up: learn it, then try it.** Seven moves, one at a time. First you do the move a few times while the camera learns your version of it: each shows up in big words with one plain line on how to do it, a timer bar, and a little figure in the corner that loops the move (it faces you like a mirror, so "left" moves to your left). Running is an easy jog (your tired pace, so tired knees still count), then a sprint with high knees; the juke is a bound left and a bound right; the truck is a half squat (your lightest hit; it sets the floor, so running never trucks by accident) and a deep squat; then stiff arm left and right, block (both arms), and jump. Each trigger is set to 50 percent of your weakest rep that still showed the move (40 percent of the jog for steps). Then you try it on a tackling dummy in the stadium, first person, with the game's own rules and the camera picture in the corner: jog to the dummy (every step the camera sees moves you, and the count shows), sprint 20 yards to it with high knees, walk up and juke it both ways, truck it (deeper hits knock it farther), stiff arm it on each side, punch the sled with both arms, jump for a lobbed ball, and hurdle the dummy lying across your path (the same jump). You stop squared up in front of the dummy, with the same beep as in the game, so there's no clock to beat. A green flash shows every rep the camera saw. A miss makes the move easier and runs it again; if the camera lost the body part the move needs (your knees for the jog, your arms for the stiff arm), you step back into frame first. After three misses the move keeps its easiest setting and the warm-up moves on, and the last screen says which moves those were. Reps in the check count toward your tuning too.
3. **Latency check.** Four clicks to count in, then one arm out to the side on each of the next four beats. The measured delay (camera plus TV mirroring) is subtracted from every timing window.

## How it's built

- One file, `index.html`, with no build step.
- The stadium, field, and players are 3D (three.js from a CDN). Players are the Quaternius mannequin (Universal Animation Library 1 and 2, CC0, in `models/`) at college sizes by position (linemen 6'4" to 6'5" and 300 plus pounds, linebackers and tight ends thick, backs compact with big legs, receivers and DBs lean), in team colors with a helmet and facemask, shoulder pads and numbers, animated with motion captured clips: a crouched stance, jog and sprint by speed, push for blocks, the throw, falls and getting up, a truck and stiff arm in replays, and a dance after touchdowns. Until the files load, or if they fail, the players built in code play instead; `?classic` in the link keeps them. The KayKit Adventurers pack can still be tried with `?kaykit`. The stadium has tiered stands with a crowd that jumps on big plays, light towers, a live scoreboard, goal posts, and fireworks on touchdowns. Your hands, timing rings, and the camera view stay 2D on top. Without WebGL, or with `?2d` added to the link, the game draws the flat 2D view instead. In body play, if tracking drops under 20 fps, the 3D view lowers its resolution in steps; it never switches to 2D in the middle of a game.
- Defense, after Ghost Reps' team pursuit: linebackers read and take on blockers, then shed; everyone takes a leading pursuit angle on where they last saw you heading; defensive backs keep a cushion and come downhill under control; defenders break down when you run at them. Bodies accelerate and turn gradually and don't run through each other.
- Contact is a small momentum model. Your speed into the hit (more when trucking) is weighed against the defender's speed and planted strength. Fast runners break more tackles, knock defenders back, and fall forward for extra yards. A defender who comes in hot can be juked clean; one who breaks down still gets a hand on you. A cut costs speed, and getting back up to speed takes a moment.
- All sound is synthesized: a stadium crowd that hushes for the snap count, stands and claps on 3rd and 4th down, and roars when you break into the open; a marching band (drumline and brass) playing the Mustang March, an original fight song, in full at halftime and as a tag after touchdowns, ducking under voices; chants and an air horn. Spoken calls use the best American male voice on the device (a second one for the booth when there is one), and you can pick one on the start screen. Studio voices (a switch on the start screen) swap in Piper, open-source neural voices that run on the device in a background worker: Ryan for the QB and Joe for the booth, both American men, a one-time download of about 63 MB each from the Hugging Face CDN (the coach voice first, so play calls are ready sooner). Lines take a fraction of a second each and are made in the gaps (never during a live play, the snap count, or the timed warm-up) and saved on the device, so later sessions start with them. Lines not made yet use the device voice. A second voice in the booth calls the big plays and does comedy about the Raiders, the band, the refs, and the crowd, never about your effort: after dark (dark and blue, the default), family, or off. The booth talks in the gaps between play calls, and the snap count waits for it to finish, so nothing gets cut off.
- Body tracking uses MediaPipe Pose Landmarker (lite model, capped at 30 fps) from a CDN.
- The shared tracking lives in one marked `<script>` block, **body core v0.7**, so it can be copied into other games:
  - Body heights (hip drop, knee rise) come from image landmarks, in torso lengths. Angles and arm heights come from world landmarks, measured against the "up" direction learned while you stand tall. Knee angle is never used.
  - Every trigger has three cues, fires when 2 of 3 cross their threshold, needs 2 of 3 to drop back before it can fire again, and has a 250 ms refractory window.
  - Triggered moves: step, hop or lateral bound (the step back to center doesn't count), lean (plus a continuous lean for steering), level change, duck, jump, arm raise (left or right), punch, rip, swim, arms overhead. Arm raises are measured in the picture's plane, which the camera sees best; punches toward the camera track poorly up close. Continuous signals: stance, cadence, lateral offset, shoulder turn.
  - The game calls `BodyCore.update()` every frame, drains events with `BodyCore.drain()`, and reads `BodyCore.state`.
